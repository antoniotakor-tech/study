01 Context

The waste is measured. The behaviour isn't.

National waste statistics are precise to the kilogram. What happens in the ten seconds between finishing a drink and letting go of the cup is not measured at all — and that is where the sorting decision is actually made.

Statistics Netherlands reports household waste per inhabitant by stream every year. Residual waste is still the largest single stream that residents themselves hand over unsorted: 149 kg per person in 2024, against 91 kg of organic waste and 40 kg of paper and cardboard.

The part nobody mentions

About 60% of Dutch municipalities now sort residual waste after collection. In much of the country a machine re-does part of the job residents are asked to do by hand — while the person at the bin gets no signal either way. Careful sorting and careless sorting feel identical at the moment of the decision.

02 Question

Is the constraint knowledge, distance, or feedback?

Campaigns almost always assume the problem is knowledge — so they explain sorting again. If the real constraint is distance or the absence of feedback, more explaining changes nothing.

The research literature is blunt about this. A study of undergraduates that compared on-site observation against the same students' self-reports found a measurable gap between stated recycling commitment and actual behaviour on campus. Willingness and action turn out to be predicted by different factors, which is why a survey asking people whether they care produces numbers that don't predict anything.

Main question
What is the binding constraint on correct waste separation by students on campus — knowledge, physical distance to a correct bin, or the absence of any feedback on the outcome?
Sub-question 1
How large is the gap between reported and observed separation behaviour on this campus?
Sub-question 2
How well calibrated is students' confidence in their sorting against a measured knowledge test?
Sub-question 3
Which intervention do students choose when forced to trade it off against a cheaper, lazier alternative?
Hypothesis
The binding constraint is situational, not attitudinal. Students will report separating far more often than the bins show; self-rated confidence will not predict measured sorting knowledge; and when forced to choose, students will name a knowledge fix while behaving as though distance is what actually binds.
03 Method

Three instruments, deliberately triangulated

No single instrument here is trusted on its own. Self-reports are checked against observation, and both are checked against what people do when asked to give something up.

  1. Bin audit — observation

    Three paired bin stations, sampled on three fixed passes a day: 11:00 (after the morning break), 13:30 (after lunch) and 16:00 (end of teaching). On each pass every item in the separated bin is scored correct or incorrect for that stream. Count items, not weight — weight is confounded by leftover liquid and needs scales you won't have. Target a minimum of 100 scored items per station before quoting a rate. Photograph each bin before sorting, as an audit trail you can put in the appendix. On the first pass two people score independently so you can report inter-rater agreement rather than assert it.

    Stations and dates: to be recorded.

  2. Survey — recalled behaviour, knowledge, and a forced trade-off

    Fourteen items. Asks what happened to the last container the respondent held, tests sorting knowledge objectively against self-rated confidence, and forces a choice between the intervention and lazier alternatives. Ends with a costly signal rather than a satisfaction rating. Distributed by QR code at the bin stations and in class channels; responses are anonymous unless a respondent opts into the pilot.

  3. Intercept interviews

    Short interviews at the bin, immediately after the decision, while the reason is still available to the person. Ninety seconds, five questions, no notebook in their face: what did you just throw away · which bin did you use · did you consider another one · what would have made the other bin easier · do you know what happens to it after this. Aim for 15–20 across the three stations and both a busy and a quiet period.

    Count and locations: to be recorded.

Prediction 2

Confidence will not predict knowledge

Q8 asks how confident people are that they sort correctly; Q9–Q11 then test it. Both the drink carton and the plastic fork are designed to catch a specific wrong mental model — “it looks like paper, so it is paper” and “it is plastic, so it is PMD”. Expect high confidence alongside low scores, with those two items producing most of the errors.

Instrument
Survey Q8 against the Q9–Q11 knowledge score
Predicted
Mean confidence ≥ 3.5 / 5, mean score ≤ 1.5 / 3, correlation weak (|r| < 0.3)
Falsified if
Confidence and score correlate at r ≥ 0.5 — people would then know what they don't know.

Prediction 3

The barrier named will be situational, not attitudinal

“No bin nearby”, “in a hurry” and “didn't think about it” describe a situation. “Don't care” and “felt pointless” describe an attitude. Campaigns are built for the second group; the prediction is that the first group is far larger, which would mean most campaigns are aimed at the wrong people.

Instrument
Survey Q6 (all that applied) and Q7 (the one that mattered most)
Falsified if
“Don't care” or “felt pointless” is the modal answer to Q7.

Prediction 4

People will misdiagnose their own constraint

This is the sharpest of the four. Q12 reveals behaviour — 5 m to the wrong bin against 40 m to the right one. Q13 asks which single change would move them. The prediction is that a majority take the near bin while a majority also ask for clearer pictures: behaving as though distance binds, while naming knowledge as the fix. If that holds, asking students what they want would have produced the wrong intervention.

Instrument
Survey Q12 (revealed) against Q13 (stated)
Falsified if
“A separated bin within 10 m of every exit” is the modal answer to Q13 — students would then be diagnosing themselves correctly.

A prediction that survives contact with data is a finding. One that doesn't is a better finding. If prediction 4 fails, write that up — it is the most interesting outcome available to this study, and it is worth more than four confirmations.

How might we…

  • Knowledge framing. How might we make the correct bin obvious in the two seconds someone spends deciding, without asking them to read anything?
  • Distance framing. How might we make the correct bin the nearest bin, rather than the virtuous detour?
  • Feedback framing. How might we make the consequence of sorting visible to the person who sorted, on a timescale they can feel?

06 Validation

The survey does not ask whether you'd use it

“Would you use this?” is the question most student projects run on, and it is close to worthless — especially here.

Recycling is the textbook case of the value–action gap: stated environmental intention systematically overstates behaviour, and asking classmates face to face adds social desirability on top of it. A survey built that way reliably returns a large majority saying yes, and predicts nothing about whether a single bin gets used correctly.

So the instrument is built to measure four things that are harder to fake:

  • Recalled behaviour, not intention. What happened to the last container you actually held, including the honest option — nearest bin, whichever one it was.
  • Measured knowledge against self-rated confidence. Three objective items after a confidence rating, so the gap between believing and knowing is a number rather than an impression.
  • A forced trade-off. The intervention is put against a cheaper, lazier alternative, because anything rated in isolation rates well.
  • A costly signal. The final question asks for something real — joining a two-week pilot — because giving something up is evidence and clicking “yes, good idea” is not.

Known limitation. Recalled behaviour is still self-reported and subject to memory error. It is checked against the bin audit rather than trusted on its own — and where the two disagree, the audit wins.
