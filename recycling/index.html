01 Context

The waste is measured. The behaviour isn't.

National waste statistics are precise to the kilogram. What happens in the ten seconds between finishing a drink and letting go of the cup is not measured at all — and that is where the sorting decision is actually made.

Statistics Netherlands reports household waste per inhabitant by stream every year. Residual waste is still the largest single stream that residents themselves hand over unsorted: 149 kg per person in 2024, against 91 kg of organic waste and 40 kg of paper and cardboard.

The part nobody mentions

About 60% of Dutch municipalities now sort residual waste after collection. In much of the country a machine re-does part of the job residents are asked to do by hand — while the person at the bin gets no signal either way. Careful sorting and careless sorting feel identical at the moment of the decision.

02 Question

Is the constraint knowledge, distance, or feedback?

Campaigns almost always assume the problem is knowledge — so they explain sorting again. If the real constraint is distance or the absence of feedback, more explaining changes nothing.

The research literature is blunt about this. A study of undergraduates that compared on-site observation against the same students' self-reports found a measurable gap between stated recycling commitment and actual behaviour on campus. Willingness and action turn out to be predicted by different factors, which is why a survey asking people whether they care produces numbers that don't predict anything.

Main question
What is the binding constraint on correct waste separation by students on campus — knowledge, physical distance to a correct bin, or the absence of any feedback on the outcome?
Sub-question 1
How large is the gap between reported and observed separation behaviour on this campus?
Sub-question 2
How well calibrated is students' confidence in their sorting against a measured knowledge test?
Sub-question 3
Which intervention do students choose when forced to trade it off against a cheaper, lazier alternative?
Hypothesis
Stated intention will exceed observed behaviour by a wide margin, and self-rated confidence will not correlate with measured sorting knowledge.
03 Method

Three instruments, deliberately triangulated

No single instrument here is trusted on its own. Self-reports are checked against observation, and both are checked against what people do when asked to give something up.

  1. Bin audit — observation

    Contents of paired bins in one building, sampled at fixed times, scored for contamination by stream. Measures behaviour with no self-report involved.
    Locations, dates and sample size to be recorded.

  2. Survey — recalled behaviour, knowledge, and a forced trade-off

    Fourteen items. Asks what happened to the last container the respondent held, tests sorting knowledge objectively against self-rated confidence, and forces a choice between the intervention and lazier alternatives. Ends with a costly signal rather than a satisfaction rating.

  3. Intercept interviews

    Short interviews at the bin, immediately after the decision, while the reason is still available to the person.
    Interview guide and count to be recorded.

06 Validation

The survey does not ask whether you'd use it

“Would you use this?” is the question most student projects run on, and it is close to worthless — especially here.

Recycling is the textbook case of the value–action gap: stated environmental intention systematically overstates behaviour, and asking classmates face to face adds social desirability on top of it. A survey built that way reliably returns a large majority saying yes, and predicts nothing about whether a single bin gets used correctly.

So the instrument is built to measure four things that are harder to fake:

  • Recalled behaviour, not intention. What happened to the last container you actually held, including the honest option — nearest bin, whichever one it was.
  • Measured knowledge against self-rated confidence. Three objective items after a confidence rating, so the gap between believing and knowing is a number rather than an impression.
  • A forced trade-off. The intervention is put against a cheaper, lazier alternative, because anything rated in isolation rates well.
  • A costly signal. The final question asks for something real — joining a two-week pilot — because giving something up is evidence and clicking “yes, good idea” is not.

Known limitation. Recalled behaviour is still self-reported and subject to memory error. It is checked against the bin audit rather than trusted on its own — and where the two disagree, the audit wins.
